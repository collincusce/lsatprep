# RC — Question types (applicable across all genres)

Each RC passage gets ~7 questions distributed across the 8 recurring types below. **Coverage guideline per passage:** 1 `main_point`, 1 `author_attitude` or `passage_structure`, 3–4 `specific_detail` or `inference`, 1 `function` or `application`, 0–1 `strengthen_weaken_claim`. Exact mix determined by passage content — don't force a type that doesn't fit. Comparative passages skew toward `specific_detail` ("mentioned in B but not A"), `author_attitude` ("authors would disagree over"), and `application` ("evidence referred to in passage B") formats that explicitly invoke the cross-passage structure.

All 8 types draw from dossier §4.2 (lines 318–329). The sample passages (`docs/research/lsac-samples/rc-*.json`) are the authoritative examples of LSAC voice for each type.

---

## main_point

**Structure.** Asks the student to select an answer that captures the **overall thesis or primary purpose** of the passage. "Main point" stems want the thesis (a content summary); "primary purpose" stems want the author's rhetorical intent (to clarify, contrast, refute, describe). Correct answers capture the full passage, not one paragraph.

**Stem templates.**
- "Which one of the following most accurately states the main point of the passage?"
- "The primary purpose of the passage is most likely to..."
- Comparative: "Which one of the following questions is central to both passages?"

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Thesis is stated explicitly in paragraph 1 or in the final sentence; one correct choice matches clearly.
- **2★:** Thesis is distributed across two paragraphs and the correct choice integrates both strands (aboriginal rights `rc-9004`: general language + inconsistent interpretation). Traps include paragraph-level restatements.
- **3★:** Thesis is only inferable from the pattern of the author's concessions and qualifications; comparative passages where the central question is one neither passage states outright.

**Trap patterns.**
- **Paragraph-level restatement:** accurately summarizes paragraph 1 or 3 but not the whole passage (dossier §4.2, line 322).
- **Too narrow:** fixates on the illustrative case rather than the broader claim.
- **Too broad / too strong:** generalizes beyond what the author argues (aboriginal rights `rc-9004` choice D "used by some provincial courts to limit these rights" overstates intent).
- **Restates a suggestion as the main point:** picks up the author's closing suggestion and labels it the thesis (aboriginal rights `rc-9004` choice E).

## author_attitude

**Structure.** Asks for the author's tone, stance, or evaluative posture toward a subject, view, or phenomenon. The correct answer captures **both the valence and the intensity** accurately — authors on the LSAT are rarely hostile or rapturous; they are "cautiously approving," "skeptical but open," "respectful but critical."

**Stem templates.**
- "Which one of the following best captures the author's attitude toward X?"
- "The author's attitude toward X can best be described as..."
- Comparative: "The authors of the two passages would be most likely to disagree over..."

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Attitude expressed directly (approving adverbs, explicit endorsement or criticism).
- **2★:** Attitude inferable from stacked qualifiers — the Lichtenstein anchor's "deliberate naiveté," "inner sweetness," "faith in reconciliation" all signal admiration for the reconciliation of realism and naiveté (`rc-9001`, correct E).
- **3★:** Attitude split across two subjects (author approves of one, disapproves of another); or, in comparative, a disagreement the student must locate among several plausible ones (global warming `rc-9010`, correct D).

**Trap patterns.**
- **Overstated positivity/negativity:** "enthusiasm" or "contempt" where the author is mildly approving or mildly skeptical.
- **Wrong feature praised/criticized:** captures the right valence but the wrong aspect (Lichtenstein `rc-9001` choice A praises "rebellion" when the passage's admiration is for the integration).
- **Mixing two attitudes when only one is present** (dossier §4.2, line 323).
- **Agreement in disguise (comparative):** proposed disagreement is something both authors would accept (global warming `rc-9010` choice B).

## passage_structure

**Structure.** Asks the student to describe the passage's **organization** — the sequence of rhetorical moves across paragraphs. Correct answers operate at paragraph grain, not sentence grain.

**Stem templates.**
- "Which one of the following most accurately describes the organization of the passage?"
- "The passage proceeds by..."
- Comparative: "Which one of the following most accurately describes the relationship between the two passages?"

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Straightforward "introduces X, explains X, gives an example, concludes" structure; answer choices differ clearly in their verbs.
- **2★:** "Introduces a view, presents a counter, concedes a point, reasserts" — subtle differences between answer choices about which paragraph does which.
- **3★:** Structures with an embedded concession or a parenthetical digression that a wrong answer might promote to a main move. In comparative, answer choices describe the A-to-B relationship; student must discriminate among related framings (point/counterpoint vs. generalization/instance vs. principle/application).

**Trap patterns.**
- **Mis-sequencing:** the moves are listed in the wrong paragraph order (dossier §4.2, line 324).
- **Wrong grain-size:** describes sentence structure rather than paragraph structure.
- **Plausible but unused move:** lists a rhetorical move (e.g., "refutes a counterexample") the passage never makes.

## specific_detail

**Structure.** Asks for something **explicitly stated** in the passage — a fact, claim, or definition. Correct answers are paraphrases; wrong answers often alter a qualifier, quantity, or scope word. In comparative, asks which passage mentions a feature.

**Stem templates.**
- "According to the passage, which one of the following..."
- "The passage states that..."
- "The phrase 'X' as used in [location] most nearly means..."
- Comparative: "Which one of the following is mentioned in passage B but not in passage A...?"

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Fact located in a single sentence; correct answer is a close paraphrase.
- **2★:** "Word in context" questions where the correct meaning is the one the passage actually uses, not the most common dictionary sense (Dutch tulip `rc-9006`: "standard pricing pattern" means "regularly recurs," not "exemplary").
- **3★:** Comparative detail questions requiring cross-passage tracking (global warming `rc-9009`: present in B, absent in A); or questions where the correct detail is stated in a subordinate clause that's easy to miss.

**Trap patterns.**
- **Altered qualifier:** changes "some" to "all" or "often" to "always."
- **Wrong sentence:** quotes verbatim language from a different part of the passage.
- **Common-meaning trap (word-in-context):** picks the ordinary dictionary meaning rather than the contextual one (Dutch tulip `rc-9006` choice E "exemplar").
- **Plausible world fact:** supplies a true-sounding detail the passage never states.

## inference

**Structure.** Asks for a claim that is **supported by but not stated in** the passage. Correct answers are usually hedged; wrong answers tend to overreach.

**Stem templates.**
- "It can be inferred from the passage that..."
- "The passage most strongly suggests that..."
- "The author would be most likely to agree with which one of the following?"
- "The passage provides evidence to suggest that the author would be most likely to assent to which one of the following proposals?"

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Inference is the natural completion of a single explicit claim.
- **2★:** Inference requires combining two claims from different paragraphs. Aboriginal rights `rc-9005` is canonical: the author's sympathy for oral tradition is inferred from their flagging the documentation burden + their general approving editorial tone.
- **3★:** Inference requires recognizing what the author rejects or treats as insufficient — the correct answer is a proposal the author has **not** endorsed but **would** endorse given their stated commitments.

**Trap patterns.**
- **Overreach / too strong:** correct inferences are usually hedged ("sometimes," "in some cases"); traps use absolute language (dossier §6.5).
- **Outside scope:** plausible-sounding claim on a related topic the passage doesn't address (aboriginal rights `rc-9005` choice A on "federal laws" generally).
- **Too weak / trivially true:** inference the author would accept but that isn't supported by the passage's specific evidence.
- **Reverses author's qualification:** author approves of X "in long-standing cases"; trap has author approve of X "in all cases" (aboriginal rights `rc-9005` choice C).

## function

**Structure.** Asks about the **purpose** of a specific sentence, paragraph, reference, or example — why the author put it there. Correct answers describe a **rhetorical function** (to illustrate, to concede, to anticipate an objection, to motivate a question), not just content.

**Stem templates.**
- "The author's discussion of X serves primarily to..."
- "The reference to X (in [location]) functions in the argument to..."
- "The author most likely mentions X in order to..."

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Referenced item is an illustrative example of an immediately-preceding claim; function is straightforward.
- **2★:** Referenced item supports a claim made two or more sentences earlier. Lichtenstein `rc-9002`: the list of themes (romances, war stories, cars, lamps) supports the realism claim made above, not the parody claim made earlier (correct A).
- **3★:** Referenced item plays a dual role — both illustrating and qualifying an earlier claim — and the correct answer must capture the specific one the question targets.

**Trap patterns.**
- **Accurate content, wrong function:** correctly characterizes what the reference says but misidentifies why it's in the passage (dossier §4.2, line 327).
- **Function from an adjacent paragraph:** identifies a rhetorical move the author does make, but elsewhere (Lichtenstein `rc-9002` choice C "contrast with abstract expressionism" — that contrast is in paragraph 2, not here).
- **Wrong grain:** describes the immediate sentence-level purpose when the question asks for a larger passage-level purpose.

## strengthen_weaken_claim

**Structure.** Asks how a new piece of information (hypothetical, introduced in the stem or answer choices) would affect the author's position or claim. Borrows mechanics from LR strengthen/weaken, but the claim to be strengthened/weakened comes from the passage.

**Stem templates.**
- "Which one of the following, if true, would most support the author's position regarding X?"
- "Which one of the following, if true, would most undermine the author's claim about X?"
- Comparative: "The author of passage B would be most likely to make which one of the following criticisms about the [claim] in passage A?"

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Claim is explicit; the strengthener/weakener directly affirms or contradicts its main evidentiary basis.
- **2★:** Claim is one of several the author makes; student must identify which claim is being asked about before evaluating choices.
- **3★:** Cross-passage criticisms (global warming `rc-9012`: B's "need better knowledge of the hydrological cycle" criticism applied to A's sea-level predictions, correct D). Student must hold both passages' specific commitments in mind and pick the criticism B would actually voice, not one a general skeptic might voice.

**Trap patterns.**
- **Off-target claim:** affects a neighboring claim in the passage, not the one the stem specifies (dossier §4.2, line 328).
- **Wrong direction:** weakens when the stem asks to strengthen, or vice versa (dossier §6.4).
- **Criticism from outside the other passage (comparative):** a reasonable criticism that B's *text* doesn't actually license, even if a like-minded skeptic would make it.
- **Overreach:** the "fact" in the answer choice is extreme enough to weaken *any* claim; the question asks specifically about the author's claim.

## application

**Structure.** Asks the student to match a **principle, pattern, or analogy** from the passage to a **new scenario** in the answer choices. The correct answer mirrors the passage's key relationship; wrong answers share surface features but diverge on the load-bearing mechanism.

**Stem templates.**
- "Which one of the following situations most closely conforms to the principle described in the passage?"
- "Which one of the following is most analogous to X as described in the passage?"
- Comparative: "Which one of the phenomena cited in passage A is an instance of the kind of 'evidence' referred to in passage B?"

**Difficulty anchors.**
- **1★:** Principle is explicit; one answer transparently instantiates it and others don't.
- **2★:** Principle requires extracting an abstraction from a specific case (Dutch tulip `rc-9007`: the mechanism is "pay high for an original asset whose value comes from low-priced descendants"; correct D is the publisher/book example).
- **3★:** Multiple answers share some features of the target principle; student must identify the specific load-bearing feature. Cross-passage application (global warming `rc-9011`: match B's "evidence of melting" to A's specific phenomenon, correct A — Larsen ice shelf).

**Trap patterns.**
- **Surface similarity:** shares topic or vocabulary with the passage but instantiates a different mechanism (Dutch tulip `rc-9007` choice C "motorcycle parts + cheap substitutes" — substitution, not reproduction).
- **Matches one dimension but not the principle's main operation** (dossier §4.2, line 329).
- **Inverts the direction:** applies the principle backwards (the analogous scenario has the same parts in a different causal order).
- **Plausible but over-generic:** any "asset bought high, sold low" example if the passage's principle is specifically about reproductive value.
